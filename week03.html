Week 03

3D Modeling and Printing - Design, slicing and FDM printing on the FLSUN

⚠️Resin safety: read this before printing with resin→

Introduction

During the third week we studied the fundamentals of three-dimensional printing, its main applications, and its usefulness in different areas of society. This technology turns digital models into physical objects through layer-by-layer fabrication, which makes it easier to build prototypes, parts, and customized models.

It is a useful tool in design, engineering, education, and product development, because it helps us visualize ideas, evaluate shapes, and materialize designs before producing them on a larger scale. This week we could finally apply the knowledge acquired in previous weeks: we created our own designs, improved them, and printed them using the Ultimaker Cura slicer.

3. Slicing in Ultimaker Cura

From 3D Model to Printer Instructions

First, the slicer is opened (Ultimaker Cura in our case) and a design file in STL format is loaded, either our own design or one found online. Then the necessary settings are chosen: infill, supports (tree-type or everywhere), wall thickness, bed adhesion, and so on.

This step matters because it converts the three-dimensional model into instructions that the printer can interpret. A good setup helps the object come out with the planned dimensions and lets us anticipate the approximate print time and material use.

The setup in Cura was necessary because the university has several 3D printers with different characteristics. Before starting, we select the right machine (here, FLSUN V400 or UltiMaker S6) and adjust the parameters to its specifications and to the needs of the design.

4. Preparing and Printing

From the File to the Machine

Having several machines gives students the chance to practice with different technologies and to learn that not all models are prepared in the same way. Checking the compatibility between the file, the material, and the printer helps prevent errors and makes better use of the laboratory resources.

The basic procedure to use a 3D printer was explained in class. Before printing, we check the following:

  1. The machine is in proper working condition.
  2. The print surface is prepared.
  3. The material and the settings are correctly selected.
  4. The file is saved from Cura to a flash drive (USB).
  5. The flash drive is connected to the FLSUN V400 screen, the file is selected, the printer is configured, and the print starts.

These checks help reduce failures, avoid wasted material, and obtain a result that matches the digital design. As a final practice, we sent one of our designs to print, which covered the whole workflow from the digital model to the start of its physical production.

6. Digital Scanning and Mesh Cleanup

From a Face Scan to a Printable Model

In the second part of this unit we moved from designing a model from scratch to capturing a real object: a face scan. A scanner records the surface as a mesh made of thousands of small triangles, but that raw mesh is not ready to print yet.

The scan was then opened in Blender to clean the mesh. The usual steps are:

  1. Import the scan and check its scale and orientation.
  2. Delete the noise: loose fragments, floating pieces, and the background captured by the scanner.
  3. Fill holes and repair broken geometry so the mesh is closed (watertight).
  4. Smooth rough areas and reduce the number of faces if the file is too heavy, without losing facial detail.
  5. Cut a flat base so the model sits correctly on the build plate.
  6. Export the result as an STL file for the slicer.
Why it matters: a slicer needs a closed mesh to tell what is inside and what is outside the model. A scan with holes or loose pieces can fail or print with defects.

7. Resin Printing

SLA / MSLA: Liquid Resin Cured by UV Light

Unlike FDM, resin printers do not melt plastic. They cure a liquid photopolymer resin with UV light, layer by layer, which gives much finer detail and smoother surfaces. That is why resin was the natural choice for the face scan.

The workflow has more steps than printing in PLA:

  1. Slice the STL in the resin slicer: tilt the model and add supports.
  2. Check the resin vat and the build plate, then start the print wearing gloves.
  3. Remove the print carefully when it finishes (it is still uncured and sticky).
  4. Wash it in isopropyl alcohol (IPA) to remove the liquid resin.
  5. Remove the supports.
  6. Post-cure it under UV light until it is fully hard.
⚠️

8. Resin Safety

Uncured resin is a skin irritant and can cause allergic reactions. Treat every step as hazardous until the part is washed and fully cured.

🧤

Protective equipment

  • Nitrile gloves, never latex.
  • Safety glasses against splashes.
  • Lab coat or apron to protect skin and clothes.
  • Change gloves when they get contaminated; do not reuse them.
💨

Ventilation

  • Print in a well-ventilated area, ideally with extraction.
  • Resin vapors can irritate the airways.
  • Keep the vat covered and bottles sealed when not in use.
🚿

Skin or eye contact

  • Skin: wash right away with soap and plenty of water.
  • Eyes: rinse with water and get medical attention.
  • Tell the lab instructor and check the resin Safety Data Sheet (SDS).
🧴

Handling and IPA

  • Fresh prints are uncured and sticky: keep gloves on until they are washed and fully cured.
  • IPA is flammable: keep it away from flames and sparks.
  • Store resin sealed, away from heat and light.
♻️

Waste disposal

  • Never pour liquid resin down the drain.
  • Cure leftover resin under UV light or sunlight until it is solid.
  • Cure contaminated gloves and paper towels before throwing them away.
  • Follow the lab rules for used IPA.

✔ Always

  • Wear gloves and safety glasses from the first to the last step.
  • Wipe spills immediately with paper towels.
  • Wash and fully UV-cure a print before touching it bare-handed.
  • Wash your hands after taking the gloves off.
  • Ask the instructor if you are not sure about a step.

✘ Never

  • Touch liquid resin or a fresh print with bare hands.
  • Use latex gloves.
  • Pour resin down the drain or throw it away uncured.
  • Eat or drink in the printing area.
  • Touch door handles, phones, or keyboards with resin-stained gloves.
If resin touches you: wash with soap and plenty of water right away, rinse your eyes with water if it splashes into them, and tell the lab instructor. Keep the resin SDS at hand.

Comparison Table

We printed in both technologies this week, so we compared them side by side. The last column shows which one wins for each criterion.

CriterionPLA (FDM)Resin (SLA/MSLA)Better
TechnologyMelts filament and deposits it layer by layer (FDM).Cures liquid resin with UV light, layer by layer (SLA/MSLA).Depends

Post-processingRemove supports; sanding or painting is optional.Wash in IPA, remove supports, and UV-cure (mandatory).PLA
StrengthTough enough for everyday and functional parts.Standard resin is hard but more brittle; it can crack.PLA

My criterion: there is no single winner. PLA is better for almost everything practical: it is cheaper, safer, faster to prepare, and prints larger parts. Resin wins when visual detail matters, as with the face scan. My rule: start with PLA, and use resin only when the detail justifies the extra cost, post-processing, and safety care.

10. Results Review

What Went Well and What Went Wrong

✔ What went well

  • The full workflow, from digital model to physical part, worked, and the checks before printing helped avoid failures.
  • The PLA keychains printed quickly and cleanly, because flat designs do not need supports.
  • The Gengar–Batman figure turned out well with supports and painted details.
  • Cleaning the scan in Blender turned a raw mesh into a printable model.
  • Resin captured the details of the face much better than PLA could.

✘ What went wrong or can improve

  • The raw scan had noise and holes, so the cleanup took more time than expected.
  • Resin needs more steps (washing, curing) and is messier; it demands strict use of protective equipment.
  • Supports leave marks in both technologies, so the pieces need sanding or painting.
  • Resin is more expensive, so a failed print costs more; it is better to test small pieces first.
  • Not every model is ready to print as it is: orientation and supports must be planned before slicing.

Conclusion

This week we went through the complete FDM 3D printing workflow: getting to know the FLSUN printer and its technical data, creating or choosing our own models, preparing them in Ultimaker Cura, checking the machine, and sending the files to print. The results, such as the Gengar–Batman figure and the Harry Potter and dog keychains printed in PLA, show how a digital design becomes a physical part.

The main lesson is that the quality of a printed piece depends on every step: the design, the slicer settings (infill, supports, layer height), the preparation of the machine, and the compatibility between the file, the material, and the printer. These practices are the base for the next weeks of Fab Academy.

We also scanned a face, cleaned the mesh in Blender, and printed it in resin. Comparing PLA and resin showed that each one has its place: PLA for fast, cheap, and large parts; resin for fine detail, as long as the safety rules are followed strictly.
