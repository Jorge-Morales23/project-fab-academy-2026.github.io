Week 03

3D Modeling and Printing, Digital Scanning and Modeling - FDM printing, 3D scanning and resin printing

Introduction

During the third week we studied the fundamentals of three-dimensional printing, its main applications, and its usefulness in different areas of society. This technology turns digital models into physical objects through layer-by-layer fabrication, which makes it easier to build prototypes, parts, and customized models.

It is a useful tool in design, engineering, education, and product development, because it helps us visualize ideas, evaluate shapes, and materialize designs before producing them on a larger scale. This week we could finally apply the knowledge acquired in previous weeks: we created our own designs, improved them, and printed them using the Ultimaker Cura slicer.

FDM 3D printingDigital scanningResin printing
Objective: go from a digital model to a physical part (design, slice, and print), and then scan a real object, edit the scan, and print it in resin.

3. Slicing in Ultimaker Cura

From 3D Model to Printer Instructions

First, the slicer is opened (Ultimaker Cura in our case) and a design file in STL format is loaded, either our own design or one found online. Then the necessary settings are chosen: infill, supports (tree-type or everywhere), wall thickness, bed adhesion, and so on.

This step matters because it converts the three-dimensional model into instructions that the printer can interpret. A good setup helps the object come out with the planned dimensions and lets us anticipate the approximate print time and material use.

The setup in Cura was necessary because the university has several 3D printers with different characteristics. Before starting, we select the right machine (here, FLSUN V400 or UltiMaker S6) and adjust the parameters to its specifications and to the needs of the design.

4. Preparing and Printing

From the File to the Machine

Having several machines gives students the chance to practice with different technologies and to learn that not all models are prepared in the same way. Checking the compatibility between the file, the material, and the printer helps prevent errors and makes better use of the laboratory resources.

The basic procedure to use a 3D printer was explained in class. Before printing, we check the following:

  1. The machine is in proper working condition.
  2. The print surface is prepared.
  3. The material and the settings are correctly selected.
  4. The file is saved from Cura to a flash drive (USB).
  5. The flash drive is connected to the FLSUN V400 screen, the file is selected, the printer is configured, and the print starts.

These checks help reduce failures, avoid wasted material, and obtain a result that matches the digital design. As a final practice, we sent one of our designs to print, which covered the whole workflow from the digital model to the start of its physical production.

6. 3D Scanning

Facial Scan with CR Studio

3D scanning records the geometry of an object and generates a digital representation that can later be reviewed, edited, or prepared for fabrication. The quality of the result depends, among other factors, on the correct use of the equipment and on following the instructions during the capture.

Our team chose Steven as the participant to be scanned. We used a 3D scanning camera together with the CR Studio software to scan his face, following the instructions of the engineer, with the goal of obtaining a digital model of the captured surface. This practice showed us the importance of scanning in an orderly way to get a more complete representation.

After the capture, the file goes through the processing options of CR Studio edit mode: fuse, remove noise, repair, and simplify (target of 400,000 faces). The scan can require adjustments before being used in other applications.

7. Editing the Scan in Blender

The scan was imported into Blender, an application used to edit and refine three-dimensional models. There we reviewed the captured geometry and worked on correcting visible imperfections, using the sculpt mode with the Smooth brush, in order to improve the presentation and the continuity of the model surface.

Editing after the scan is important because the initial file can contain irregularities, incomplete areas, or details that need cleaning. Modeling tools let us prepare the digital object for review, export, or possible fabrication, depending on the file and on the process to be used.

8. Resin Printing

From the Scan to a Physical Piece

As a result of the editing process, we obtained a visually cleaner and more defined model. Once reviewed, the design can be exported to continue with the preparation and fabrication stages, in this case through resin printing.

This activity showed that scanning and 3D printing complement each other: first a real object is captured digitally, then the model is improved, and finally it is prepared for its possible physical reproduction. The final quality depends on the initial capture, on the editing, and on the fabrication parameters selected.

Conclusion

This week we went through the complete digital fabrication workflow: designing a model, preparing it in a slicer, checking the machine, and printing it on an FDM printer. We also learned that real objects can be captured with a 3D scanner, cleaned in modeling software, and sent to a resin printer.

The main lesson is that the quality of a printed piece depends on every step: the design, the slicer settings, the preparation of the machine, and, in the case of scanning, the care taken during capture and editing.
